Gutters & Decks in New Hampshire

Two jobs that both come down to managing water and building for a New England winter. We install seamless gutters sized for a real spring melt, and decks set on footings that go below the frost line.

What We Do

Water Management and Outdoor Living

Gutters and decks sit at opposite ends of a home improvement list, but both fail for the same reason in New Hampshire: they were built for average conditions rather than for what actually happens here in March.

Undersized gutters overflow the moment weeks of accumulated snow melt at once. Deck footings that stop short of the frost line heave out of level after a single hard winter. Both are avoidable at the point of installation and expensive to correct afterward.

Vincenzo’s Roofing handles both from our base in Seabrook. Coming at this from roofing matters more than it sounds, because the two places these jobs go wrong are drainage sizing and the flashed connection where structure meets the wall of your house.

Part One

Seamless Gutter Installation & Repair

Gutters are the least glamorous thing on your house and one of the most consequential. Everything they fail to carry away ends up against your foundation.

Assessment

Signs Your Gutters Need Replacing

01

Overflowing during heavy rain

Usually undersized gutters or blocked downspouts. Water sheeting over the front edge is doing nothing useful for your foundation.

02

Pulling away from the fascia

Ice weight and rotted fascia board loosen the hangers. Once a section sags it stops draining toward the downspout.

03

Leaking at the seams

Sectional gutters fail at their joints first. Seamless runs remove that problem across the whole length of the house.

04

Standing water after rain

Water sitting in the trough means the pitch is wrong. It will freeze in winter and add serious weight to the run.

05

Erosion or pooling at the foundation

Channels in the mulch and damp basement walls mean water is landing where it should have been carried away.

06

Peeling paint or rotted trim

Water running behind the gutter rots fascia and soffit, which then fails to hold the gutter properly. It compounds.

Options

Gutter Systems We Install

Sizing is the decision that matters most here, and it is the one most quotes skip over entirely.

Standard

5 inch seamless

K style aluminum formed on site to your exact run

  • No joints along the run, so no seams to leak
  • Suits most New Hampshire homes and roof areas
  • Wide range of colors to match trim or siding
  • Hidden hangers screwed into solid fascia
Service life: 20 to 30 years

Heavy runoff

6 inch seamless

Oversized profile with larger downspouts

  • Carries roughly 40 percent more water than 5 inch
  • Recommended for large or steeply pitched roofs
  • Handles valleys that concentrate runoff into one section
  • Noticeably better through a fast spring melt
Service life: 20 to 30 years

Add on

Gutter guards

Mesh or surface tension covers over the trough

  • Cuts cleaning to roughly once a year under trees
  • Keeps downspouts clear ahead of heavy melt
  • Several grades available at different price points
  • Not a fix for ice dams, whatever the marketing says
Best for: homes with overhanging trees

An honest word about ice dams

No gutter or guard product prevents ice damming. Ice dams are caused by warm attic air melting snow on the roof, which then refreezes at the cold eave. Gutters simply happen to be where the ice becomes visible. The actual fix is attic insulation and balanced ventilation, and any company selling guards as an ice dam solution is telling you something that is not true.

Part Two

Deck Building & Replacement

A deck is a structure fastened to your house and standing in ground that freezes four feet deep. Built properly it lasts decades. Built quickly it moves, sags, and lets water into the wall.

What We Build

Deck Work We Take On

01

New deck construction

Designed around your house and your grade level, permitted with the town, and built to current code.

02

Full deck replacement

Old structure removed to the footings, which are assessed and replaced where they have heaved or cracked.

03

Resurfacing over sound framing

Where the frame is solid, new decking and railings give you a new deck at a fraction of a full rebuild.

04

Ledger repair and reflashing

The most common failure we are called for, and the one that quietly rots the rim joist of the house.

05

Railings and stairs

Code compliant railing heights and baluster spacing, plus stairs built to consistent rise and run.

06

Repairs and board replacement

Soft boards, loose fasteners, and wobbly rail posts put right before they become a safety problem.

Materials

Choosing Your Decking

The decision comes down to how much maintenance you are willing to do each spring.

Budget

Pressure treated

Treated softwood, the traditional New England choice

  • Lowest material cost by a wide margin
  • Readily available and simple to repair board by board
  • Needs cleaning and resealing every one to two years
  • Will check and splinter with age if left unsealed
Service life in NH: 15 to 20 years

Most popular

Composite

Wood fiber and polymer boards with a capped surface

  • No sanding, sealing, or staining, ever
  • Resists fading, staining, and splintering
  • Hidden fastener systems give a clean surface
  • Costs two to three times pressure treated up front
Service life in NH: 25 to 30 years

Natural

Cedar

Naturally rot resistant softwood with real grain

  • Warm natural appearance that suits older homes
  • Naturally resistant to rot and insects
  • Stays cooler underfoot than composite in summer
  • Needs regular sealing, more so near the coast
Service life in NH: 15 to 25 years

Scope of Work

How We Build a Deck for This Climate

  • Footings dug to 48 inches, below the New Hampshire frost line
  • Concrete piers with proper post bases, no wood in soil
  • Ledger board bolted to structure, never nailed to sheathing
  • Ledger flashed above and behind to keep the rim joist dry
  • Joist spacing set for the decking material specified
  • Joist tape over framing tops to extend frame life
  • Hot dipped galvanized or stainless structural connectors
  • Coastal grade fasteners on Seacoast properties
  • Railing heights and baluster spacing to current code
  • Stairs built to consistent rise and run with solid footing
  • Town permit pulled and inspections scheduled
  • Site cleared and written workmanship warranty registered

Straight Pricing

How Much Does a Deck Cost in New Hampshire?

$6,000 to $30,000

Pressure treated on a simple low deck sits near the bottom of that range. Composite on an elevated deck with railings, stairs, and lighting sits at the top. Height above grade moves the number more than most people expect, because it changes the footing, framing, and railing requirements.

  • Square footage the finished deck area
  • Decking material pressure treated, cedar, or composite
  • Height above grade taller decks need more structure and railing
  • Railing choice wood, composite, cable, or aluminum
  • Stairs number of runs and landings required
  • Site conditions ledge, slope, and access for equipment

Local Expertise

What New Hampshire Demands From Both

These four conditions are why work built to a generic spec fails here and work built for this climate does not.

A four foot frost line

Ground here freezes deep. Footings that stop above the frost line heave every winter, pulling a deck out of level and stressing the ledger connection to the house. Forty eight inches is the standard we build to, confirmed with your town at permit stage.

Spring melt in a single weekend

Weeks of accumulated snow can release over two or three warm days. That is the load your gutters actually have to carry, not an average rainfall, which is why 6 inch profile is worth pricing on larger roofs.

Ice weight on the eaves

A frozen gutter run is extraordinarily heavy. Hangers spaced too far apart or fixed into rotted fascia will fail under it, taking the fascia board with them. Spacing and fixing substrate both matter.

Salt air on the Seacoast

Near the water, standard fasteners and connectors corrode years early. On coastal decks and gutter runs we specify stainless or hot dipped components as standard rather than as an upgrade.

FAQ

Gutter & Deck Questions

Seamless aluminum gutters on an average New Hampshire home typically cost between 1,200 and 4,500 dollars installed. The variables are the total run in linear feet, whether you choose 5 inch or 6 inch profile, the number of downspouts and how they are routed, the height of the house, and whether gutter guards are added.

Most New Hampshire homes are served well by 5 inch K style gutters. We recommend upgrading to 6 inch where the roof is large, steeply pitched, or has long valleys that concentrate runoff into one section. A 6 inch gutter carries roughly 40 percent more water, which matters most during a fast spring melt rather than during ordinary rainfall.

Most decks in New Hampshire cost between 6,000 and 30,000 dollars. Pressure treated lumber on a simple ground level deck sits at the lower end, while composite decking on an elevated deck with railings, stairs, and lighting sits at the upper end. Footing depth, height above grade, and railing choice all move the number significantly.

Composite costs roughly two to three times more up front and lasts 25 to 30 years with almost no maintenance beyond washing. Pressure treated lumber is far cheaper but needs cleaning and resealing every one to two years, and will typically last 15 to 20 years in New Hampshire weather. If you dislike annual maintenance, composite usually justifies its cost over the life of the deck.

New Hampshire has a deep frost line, and most towns require deck footings to reach 48 inches below grade so they sit beneath it. Footings that stop short will heave as the ground freezes and thaws, pulling the deck out of level and stressing the ledger connection to the house. Exact depth is set by your local building department and we confirm it when pulling the permit.

In almost all cases yes. Requirements vary by town, and most New Hampshire municipalities require a building permit for any deck attached to the house or raised above a set height. We handle the permit application and the inspections as part of the project so the deck is documented properly for insurance and for any future sale.

The ledger board is the beam that fastens the deck to the house, and it sits directly against the wall. Without proper flashing above it, water runs behind the ledger and into the rim joist and sheathing. Rotted ledger connections are a leading cause of deck failure and a common source of hidden water damage, which is why we flash every ledger as a matter of course.
